# Clear explainer writing

Start with what the reader can already do and what they should be able to do after reading. Work one real example before writing. For a Sudoku technique, identify the marks that qualify, the rule that forces the move, and the exact candidate to place or erase. Use the order that makes the reasoning easiest to check.

## Edit for the reader

- **Make the opening useful on its own.** Give the move or the thing to spot. Cut introductions that announce the subject, praise the technique, or promise what the reader will learn.
- **Put tests where the reader makes a decision.** Name what to look at and what must be true before acting. Give the missing reason between the pattern and the result. If the proof has two cases, carry both through to the result.
- **Give each part of the page a job.** A subtitle tells the reader when the technique applies. The body teaches the rule. The caption walks through the particular board. The exercise lets the reader try it. Cut a sentence if the nearby heading, paragraph, or figure already does its job.
- **Name the thing instead of reaching for a synonym.** Use house for any row, column, or box. Name the row, column, or box when its type or position matters. Keep candidate, place, and remove distinct. Define unfamiliar terms where the reader first needs them, or point to Fundamentals for shared vocabulary.
- **Put the concrete details next to the diagram.** Use the same digit and cell address as the board. A reader should be able to follow the reasoning by address even if they cannot distinguish the colors. Keep practice instructions to the next action the reader can take.

## Check for formulaic prose

- **Circle words that hide the action.** Replace abstract nouns and broad verbs such as “enable,” “facilitate,” and “supply” with the thing that changes. “The other column loses 6” tells the reader more than “This enables candidate elimination.”
- **Look for sentences that could fit any lesson.** Cut claims of importance, broad summaries, automatic transitions, rhetorical questions, and generic reassurance. Keep comparisons that explain which move to make.
- **Read neighboring sections together.** Watch for repeated sentence starters, headings with the same rhythm, and sets of three that add no information. Use short sentences for separate checks. Keep a condition and its consequence together when splitting them would obscure the connection.
- **Use patterns as prompts to reread.** Vocabulary and grammatical tendencies vary by model, subject, and audience. Judge the sentence by what it helps the reader understand.

## Verify the lesson

Read it with the actual page open. Check each variant, any uniqueness assumption, the direction of a link, and who can see a target. Check that figure text agrees with the candidate marks, that the exercise asks for a valid move, and that the wording distinguishes what must be true from what could be true.

For a pair, “R8C2 and R8C5 each have only 4 and 6. They must use both digits, so erase 4 and 6 from the other cells in row 8” gives the pattern, the reason, and the action.
